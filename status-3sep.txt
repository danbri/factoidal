Publish is running for @factoidal/core 0.3.0, workflow 33758782842, which re-extracts F* on Linux and refuses on drift before publishing.

Is it a respectable RDF store yet? As a standards engine, yes. SPARQL 1.1 at 631 pass, 0 fail, SPARQL 1.2 at 254 pass, 0 fail, the RDF 1.1 and 1.2 syntaxes complete, and a correctness story almost nobody else has: round-trip theorems for every on-disk codec, a proof that the fast join equals the reference join, proofs that the fast parser equals the specification parser.

As a store, it is early but real: Merkle-committed artifacts, a durable delta log with a crash-safety argument, atomic activation through a pointer, compaction, and now named graphs on disk.

As a database, not yet, and here is precisely what is missing.

- No server. The Lean persisted path has no SPARQL endpoint. You cannot point a client at it.
- One writer. No locking in the JavaScript path, no transactions beyond a delta batch, no isolation story.
- The planner is predicate-level. It picks blocks by constant predicate or graph name and otherwise reads everything. No statistics, no cost model, no join ordering.
- Named-graph queries read whole blocks, because the postings sidecars have no graph dimension yet.
- Scale is unproven above 3.1 million triples. Serious stores are measured at a hundred million and up. Pack is now about 12,000 triples per second, so a hundred million triples is a couple of hours, and nobody has tried it.
- No peer benchmark exists. Not one number against Jena, Oxigraph, Virtuoso or Blazegraph, and no standard workload run at all. This is the credibility gap, not the code.

The honest one-line verdict: a formally grounded, standards-complete engine with an early but genuine persistent store, and no evidence yet that it is competitive, because the comparison has never been run.

That suggests the next milestone after named-graph sidecars, and it is cheap compared to what today cost: pick one standard benchmark, run it against this engine and against one peer on the same machine, and publish both columns including the ones we lose. A benchmark we lose is worth more than a benchmark we do not run.

Meanwhile the parity supervisor is working through the OWL delta, where the Lean probe sits at 1,131 pass, 316 fail out of 1,457 against an F* engine that is near-perfect on the same ground. That is the largest single correctness gap left between the two trees.
